The problem with "trust your read"

Most golfers get the read right and the aim wrong — the ball drifts a few degrees in the ten seconds it takes to stand up and stroke it.

That's not a talent problem — it's a hand-eye handoff, and it happens to 8-handicaps and 28-handicaps alike. Jeff Sargent spent 25 years watching students make the same mistake, so he built a tool that separates the two jobs: read the break, lock the line in, then place the ball already aimed.

The six-step routine

Mark it. Read it. Aim it. Sink it.

The whole routine takes less time than re-marking your ball the old way — and every step is legal to use during a round.

01

Mark

Mark your ball from behind with the 1Putt, the same way you'd set down any ball marker.

02

Read

Walk the green from all sides and determine the break, just like you already do.

03

Aim

Keep your target line in sight and pivot the 1Putt with your putter's grip until it points down that line.

04

Place

Set the ball down, lining up the three stenciled lines with the three lines on the 1Putt.

05

Remove

Lift the 1Putt and make your stroke. The ball stays exactly where you aimed it.

06

Watch

Watch the lines roll end-over-end straight into the cup — proof your stroke matched your aim.

USGA & R&A Equipment conforming

Is this actually legal to use?

Yes — and here's the exact rule, not just our word for it.

The Equipment Rules permit a ball-marker with alignment lines as long as it's under 1 inch tall and under 2 inches in any horizontal direction. Anything larger is classified as an "alignment device" and isn't permitted during a round. The 1Putt is built inside those limits by design, so you can mark, aim, and remove it before every stroke — in practice or in competition.

The stencil marks are yours to keep on the ball. Drawing alignment lines onto a golf ball itself has never been against the Rules of Golf — it's the same reason factory three-line balls exist. The 1Putt just lets you put those lines on any ball, aimed exactly where you need them.
